Weibull Analysis — Engineering Theory
Why Weibull · Parameter meaning · Application to corrosion · How to read the plots
Why Weibull fits corrosion perfectly
The Weibull distribution was developed by Waloddi Weibull in 1951 to model material fatigue failure. It became the standard reliability engineering tool because its shape parameter β can represent all three phases of the bathtub curve:

β < 1 Infant mortality — failures decrease over time. Defects present from manufacturing.

β = 1 Random failures — constant failure rate. Memoryless (exponential).

β > 1 Wear-out — failure rate increases. Age-dependent degradation.

For salt-market automotive ground stud corrosion, β is typically 2.2–3.5. This means the failure rate is accelerating — each additional winter does proportionally more damage than the previous one. This is the physical basis for why you are in the most dangerous phase right now after 7 years.
The two-parameter Weibull model
F(t) = 1 − e^(−(t/η)^β)

Where:
F(t) = probability of failure by time t
t = time (years of service)
β (beta) = shape parameter — controls curve shape
η (eta) = scale parameter — the characteristic life (time at which F = 63.2%)

For corrosion modelling we substitute resistance for "failure":
R(t) = R₀ + A × (t/η)^β

This gives a power-law degradation model where resistance grows with service years, accelerating over time. The η scale parameter encodes the local environment — salt concentration, freeze-thaw cycles per year, drainage quality. A Toronto vehicle has a much lower η (faster degradation) than a Vancouver vehicle.
How to read the Weibull probability plot
The Weibull probability plot linearises the cumulative failure distribution by transforming both axes:

X-axis: ln(t) — log of time
Y-axis: ln(ln(1/(1-F))) — double log of unreliability

In this transformed space, a perfect Weibull fit appears as a straight line. The slope of the line is β. The x-intercept at the 63.2% unreliability level is η. Data points that curve away from the line indicate the model needs a three-parameter fit (adding a location parameter γ).

Points above the line: Degrading faster than model predicts. Aggressive intervention needed.
Points below the line: Degrading slower than model. Model is conservative — safe.
Tight scatter around line: High confidence in model. Narrow confidence intervals.
How measurement frequency affects confidence
With 2 measurements: confidence interval on RUL is ±60%. Not useful for planning.
With 3 measurements: ±35%. Enough for a directional decision.
With 4 measurements: ±20%. Adequate for maintenance scheduling.
With 6+ measurements: ±10%. Engineering-grade prediction.

This is why the annual autumn measurement protocol is specifically recommended — not because one measurement tells you something useful, but because 3 years of data (6 measurements, autumn + spring) gives you a ±20% confidence interval on the fire threshold crossing date. That is the precision you need to plan a service visit, not just react to a DTC.
Salt market adjustment factor
The Weibull η parameter is not universal. It must be calibrated to local conditions.

Toronto: 94 average freeze-thaw cycles/yr, chloride application rate ~200 kg/km. η × 0.65 (aggressive).
Vancouver: 8 freeze-thaw cycles/yr, minimal chloride. η × 1.4 (benign).
Milan: ~40 cycles/yr, moderate salt. η × 1.0 (baseline).
Southern US: <5 cycles/yr, low salt. η × 2.0 (this failure mode unlikely).

The demo data in this tracker is calibrated for Toronto. If you are in a different market, multiply all RUL projections by the local η factor ratio.
